WHAT A KALSHI TRADE REALLY COSTS

Three numbers decide whether a Kalshi trade is worth it: the fee, the probability the price is claiming, and the break-even your own forecast has to beat once the fee is in. This calculator does all three, with Kalshi's actual formula — no signup, nothing stored.

KEY INSIGHTS

  • The taker fee peaks at a 50¢ price: 1.75¢ per contract. It shrinks toward the extremes — a 5¢ or 95¢ contract costs a fraction of a cent in formula terms — because the fee is charged on expected earnings, not on the stake.
  • The price is the market's probability claim; your break-even is higher. A 37¢ contract breaks even at 37% before fees — and at 38.7% after them, on a 20-contract order. The fee moves the goalposts, not the price.
  • Maker orders pay a quarter of the taker rate (0.0175 in the formula instead of 0.07) — but only when your resting order actually fills. A cancelled resting order costs nothing.
  • There is no settlement fee and no membership fee. The trading fee on entry is the whole toll for a buy-and-hold-to-settlement trade.
  • Kalshi rounds the fee up so your fee plus position cost lands on a whole cent. On a single contract, that rounding is most of the fee.

THE FORMULA, IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Kalshi's trading fee is 7% of the contract's expected earnings — price times the room left to $1 — charged per contract and rounded up to the whole cent. In Kalshi's own notation:

fee = round up( 0.07 × contracts × price × (1 − price) )
  • Price is in dollars: a 37¢ contract is 0.37, so (1 − price) is 0.63 — the room the contract has to run.
  • Maker orders use 0.0175 instead of 0.07 — exactly a quarter of the taker rate — charged only if the resting order fills.
  • Round up means the fee is rounded up until fee + position cost lands on a whole cent. On multi-contract orders the formula dominates; on a single contract, the round-up usually sets the fee: 1¢ at most prices, 2¢ in the 20¢–80¢ band.
  • Some series carry their own fee multiplier (a few pay none). Kalshi's daily temperature markets are not on that exception list — the general formula above is the one that applies to weather trades. The full schedule, including every exception, is on Kalshi's fee schedule page.

The worked example at the top, by hand: 0.07 × 20 contracts × 0.37 × 0.63 = $0.3263…, which rounds up to a $0.33 fee. Position cost $7.40, total $7.73, and $7.73 spread over 20 contracts means each contract must pay off at least 38.65% of the time just to break even. That's the whole tool — everything else on this page is those three moves explained.

That is not an argument for living at the extremes (longshots carry their own tax, charged by probability instead of the exchange); it's an argument for counting the fee before you count your edge.

03

IMPLIED PROBABILITY: WHAT THE PRICE IS CLAIMING

A contract's price in cents is the market's probability claim in percent: 37¢ means "about 37%." That's the whole conversion, and the calculator's first output is just your price read back as a belief. Two honest caveats keep that number from lying to you:

  • It's a price, not a measurement. It moves with money and mood, not just information — the point our Beginner's Guide opens with. Late in a weather day it tracks the station closely; early, it's mostly forecast and crowd.
  • Both sides sum to more than 100%. The YES and NO prices on the same bracket add up past $1 — the gap is the spread, the market's own margin. So "the market believes 37%" really means "you can buy the market's 37% claim at 37¢; selling it back pays you less."

And once the fee is in, the market's claim isn't your hurdle anyway — your break-even is. A 37¢ buy doesn't need the event to be a 37% shot; with fees, on a small order, it needs to be a 39%-ish shot. Traders who skip that step systematically overrate every position they hold by a point or two — which compounds exactly the way you'd expect.

04

THE EV CHECK, DONE HONESTLY

Expected value asks one question: if your probability estimate is right, does this trade make money over many repetitions? The math is (your chance × $1 payout) minus your full cost including the fee. Positive means the price is cheaper than your belief; negative means you're paying more than your own forecast says the contract is worth.

Now the part most EV calculators leave out: the output is only as good as the probability you type in. Type in a wish and the tool will happily bless it. Our desk grades its own probability calls against the official settlement prints every morning — the same record published in the journal — and the standing lesson of that record is humbling: the market's price is usually close, the favorite is usually priced about right, and a claimed 8-point edge is more often a modeling error than a discovery. Use the EV check as a discipline, not a permission slip: it will reliably stop the trades where you can't even state a probability that beats the break-even — and it will faithfully green-light a bad forecast. The estimate is the trade. The calculator just does its arithmetic.

One more boundary: this tool prices a buy-and-hold-to-settlement trade. If you sell before settlement, that's a second trade with its own fee, and this math no longer describes your position. And none of it settles anything — for what actually pays you, read how these markets settle.

QUESTIONS TRADERS ASK ABOUT KALSHI FEES

How much are Kalshi's trading fees?
For most markets, the taker fee is 7% of the contract's expected earnings: 0.07 × contracts × price × (1 − price), rounded up to the whole cent. It peaks at 1.75¢ per contract at a 50¢ price and falls toward the extremes — $0.34 total on 100 contracts at 5¢, $1.75 on 100 at 50¢. Maker orders pay a quarter of that rate. A small number of series carry their own multiplier; Kalshi's daily temperature markets use the general schedule.

Is there a settlement fee on Kalshi?
No. There is no settlement fee and no membership fee. For a buy-and-hold trade, the trading fee charged when your order fills is the only exchange fee.

What's the difference between maker and taker fees?
A taker order matches immediately against the book and pays the 0.07 rate. A maker order is a limit order that rests on the book; if it later fills, it pays the 0.0175 rate — a quarter as much — and if it never fills or you cancel it, it costs nothing. The trade-off is certainty: a resting order may never fill, and the price can run away from it.

Does a contract's price equal its probability?
As a claim, yes: a 37¢ price is the market pricing the event at roughly 37%. As your break-even, no — once the trading fee is included, you need the event to happen more often than the price suggests just to come out even (38.7% on the worked example on this page).

Why is the fee on one contract sometimes the same at very different prices?
Rounding. Kalshi rounds the fee up so fee + position cost lands on a whole cent, and below a cent the formula can't go — so a single contract costs 1¢ across most of the low and high ranges and 2¢ through the middle. The curve only shows its true shape on multi-contract orders.
