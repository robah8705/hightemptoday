Across 472 November days in the official record from 2011 to 2026, Hartsfield-Jackson International's highs averaged 65.0°, with a median of 66° — but one November day in ten came in at 52° or colder, and one in ten reached 76° or warmer. That spread, a standard deviation of 8.8°, is the honest shape of a Atlanta November.

WHY ATLANTA'S OFFICIAL HIGH COMES FROM THE AIRPORT

Atlanta's official weather record is kept at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, south of downtown, at about 1,025 feet on the Piedmont plateau. It's the station the National Weather Service uses for Atlanta's daily climate report, and the one Kalshi's Atlanta high-temperature market names in its rules.

Elevation is the quiet fact of this market. Atlanta is one of the highest big cities in the East, and its airport shares that height — which shaves the top off hot days and deepens cold ones relative to the lower terrain toward the coast and the Gulf. Readings from the city's lower, northern suburbs are not readings of the same air.

ATLANTA: A PLATEAU, A WEDGE, AND A THOUSAND FEET

Atlanta grew up on a ridge of the Piedmont, the rolling upland between the Appalachians and the coastal plain. The city's weather is what that position produces: southern warmth, lifted a thousand feet, with the mountains close enough to interfere.

  • Plateau elevation. A thousand feet of altitude takes the edge off both extremes of the lowland South — Atlanta's summer peaks run below the coastal plain's, and its fall cold snaps bite harder than cities at sea level expect.
  • Cold-air damming — the wedge. When high pressure sits off the Northeast coast, shallow cold air banks up against the Appalachians and spills southwest over Georgia. Wedge days sit gray, raw and capped, sometimes ten degrees below the forecast, while stations west of the mountains are mild.
  • The urban forest. Atlanta's famous tree canopy and sprawl moderate the classic heat island — the airport's open plateau setting reads cleaner than a downtown sensor choked in asphalt and shade at once.

You can see the shape of it in the tables above: over the last 30 days, Hartsfield-Jackson International's official highs ran from 53° to 81°, averaging 68.5°. That is Atlanta's character in one line — the rest of this page is about trading it honestly.

THE WRONG-STATION TRAP: THE NORTH SIDE IS A DIFFERENT CLIMATE

  • Peachtree-DeKalb (KPDK), inside the northeast perimeter, is the app's favorite Atlanta station — closer to downtown, lower, and regularly a degree or two off the official number.
  • Fulton County (KFTY) on the west side and Dobbins ARB (KMGE) in Marietta sit higher and further into the foothills' shadow — wedge days hit them differently from the airport.
  • Downtown readings come from a canyon of buildings at a different elevation from the plateau's south edge. Color, not settlement.

Atlanta's metro drapes across ridges and valleys for fifty miles. The market picked one station on the south plateau, and it is the only one whose number prints.

WHEN ATLANTA'S NUMBER BECOMES OFFICIAL

Atlanta's climate report comes from the National Weather Service office in Peachtree City, and it lands in two steps. A preliminary report posts in the late afternoon — today's came at 8:33 PM — carrying the day's high as it stands. The final report, covering the full climate day, posts around 4:19 AM. Kalshi settles the Atlanta market on The Weather Company's reporting of that final number, in whole degrees.

Two habits worth building: never grade your own day off the preliminary alone, because the final is the one that pays; and remember the climate day runs midnight to midnight standard time, so during daylight saving the official day runs 1 AM to 1 AM on the clock — a warm midnight hour can belong to either day, and the report, not your watch, decides which.

What time of day does Atlanta usually hit its high?

On a typical fall day, between about 1:33 PM and 4:38 PM, most often in the 3:00 PM hour. 5.7% of November highs are set before noon.

Why is Atlanta's forecast sometimes off by several degrees?

Because the wedge is the usual suspect — shallow Appalachian cold air is one of the hardest patterns in the East to time, and when it arrives early or refuses to leave, the high can miss by ten degrees. In the official record, November highs at the airport average about 65°, with a standard deviation of 8.8° — that spread is the honest size of a Atlanta November surprise.
