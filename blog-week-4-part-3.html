WEEK 4, PART 3: THE HONESTY TEST

By Buzz·PUBLISHED Monday, November 23, 2026·UPDATED November 23, 2026·4 MIN READ

The final scoreboard said our probabilities lost to the market's. Here we show the test itself — the one that grades not whether our picks won, but whether the numbers we said out loud were honest. Ours weren't. The market's were.

Key insights
  • On the 87 brackets where we disagreed with the market, we said 21.1% on average. Those picks won 13.8% of the time. We were overconfident by 7.3 points.
  • The market's implied probability on those same brackets: 13.9%. Nearly exact.
  • Scored formally (Brier score, lower is better): the market 0.0922, our model 0.1267. A coin-flip forecaster who always says 50/50 scores 0.25 — we carry real signal, just less than the crowd.
  • The one place our layer made money in the study — +$22.23 across the 87 — was picking which tickets and what they cost, not having better probabilities.

WHY GRADE PROBABILITIES AT ALL

A pick can win for the wrong reason and lose for the right one. Any single day proves nothing — a bracket we price at 20% is supposed to lose four times out of five. So wins and losses alone can't tell you whether a forecast is good. Probabilities can, but only in bulk: say 20% a hundred times, and if the thing happens about twenty times, you were honest. If it happens eight times, you were flattering yourself.

That's the test we ran on ourselves — and on the market, on the exact same brackets. It's called calibration, and there's a standard score for it: the Brier score. Zero is perfect, 0.25 is what you get for shrugging "fifty-fifty" at everything, and lower is better.

THE TEST: 87 TIMES WE SAID THE MARKET WAS WRONG

The fairest arena is disagreement. When our model and the market price a bracket the same way, there's nothing to grade — and nothing to trade. So we pulled every pick from the study window (Oct 31 – Nov 9) where Bright's recentered model took a position against the market's price: 87 brackets. Then we scored both sets of numbers against what actually settled.

And the single number that explains the table: across those 87 brackets, our stated probabilities averaged 21.1%. The picks won 13.8% of the time. The market's implied probability on the identical brackets averaged 13.9%.

We thought we were finding the market's mistakes. On average, the "mistake" was ours — we were systematically more sure of ourselves than reality justified, by about seven points. The market, on the very brackets we chose to fight it on, was priced almost exactly right.

WHERE IT WENT WRONG: THE FAR BRACKETS

The study had a shape to it. Head-to-head on the 87: Bright's pick won 12 times, the original Scout pick won 5, and 70 times both lost — disagreement is a hard neighborhood to begin with.

The worst class inside it was the far switches: 45 picks where the model reached for a bracket well away from the market's center — the cap-and-tail end of the ladder. Those won 6.7% of the time and lost $45.72. The pattern has a name at this desk now: the distance problem. The further our pick sat from where the day's action actually was, the more our confidence was fiction.

And it kept confirming itself after the study window closed. In the run's final days, the genuine disagreement picks went 0-for-5, then 0-for-6. The picks that paid in that stretch were the near-settled, close-in ones — the opposite of a bold call.

THE PART THAT SAVES THE EXPERIMENT

Here's the nuance the scoreboard alone misses: across those same 87 picks, Bright's layer made money — +$22.23. Not because its probabilities were sharper; this test just proved they weren't. It made money on the unglamorous half of trading: which tickets got bought, at what price, and which fights got declined. Selection and cost, not prophecy.

That's a genuinely useful thing to learn about yourself. It says the desk's edge, such as it is, lives in discipline — the floor under prices we'll pay, the brackets we refuse to reach for — and not in out-forecasting a crowd that grades out at 13.9% against a 13.8% reality. Every change in tomorrow's post traces back to that sentence.

WHAT WE DID WITH THE ANSWER

We didn't tune the model until the test passed — that's just grading your own homework with a friendlier pen. The stated probabilities stay stated, the calibration log keeps every one of them, and the fixes went where the evidence pointed: a floor under the prices we pay, a quarantine label on picks that are dead on arrival, and a permanent market shadow on the scoreboard so the comparison never goes away.

The full graded record behind this post — every pick, every stated probability, every settlement — is the same record this site publishes as a matter of policy. If our numbers ever start flattering us again, it'll show there first.
