KEY FACTS ABOUT DENVER'S HIGH

  • One station sets the number: Denver International Airport (KDEN). Not downtown, not Boulder, not the reading on your phone.
  • Denver is the seesaw city. Its November highs average about 55°, but the spread is the story: a standard deviation of 13.4° in our 15-year archive of official highs. This week alone printed 65° on Friday and 40° on Monday.
  • It reports about every 5 minutes, so you can watch the afternoon climb — or a front erase it — in close to real time.
  • The print has two steps: a preliminary climate report around 4:30 PM Mountain, then the final around 12:30 AM. The market settles on the final.

DENVER IN LATE NOVEMBER: WHAT THE RECORD SAYS

November is Denver's wildest month to trade, and the archive shows why. Across 472 November days in the official record from 2011 to 2026, Denver International's highs averaged 55.3°, with a median of 57° — but one November day in ten came in at 37° or colder, and one in ten reached 71° or warmer. The month doesn't have a typical day; it has a typical range, and the range is enormous.

This November has run warm: through November 24, the month's official highs average 59.0°, about 3.7° above that 15-year November average. Zoom in on this exact stretch of the calendar — November 20 through 30, over the last seven years — and the official highs ran from 21° to 72°, with a median of 51°. If a forecast for this week looks confident, it hasn't met Denver in late November.

THE RULEIn Denver, price the range, not the point. A November forecast is a center of a very wide distribution, and the brackets near the middle are where that distribution is flattest.

WHY DENVER'S OFFICIAL HIGH COMES FROM THE AIRPORT

Denver's official weather record is kept at Denver International Airport, on the plains northeast of the city at 5,434 feet. It's the station the National Weather Service uses for Denver's daily climate report, and the one Kalshi's Denver high-temperature market names in its rules. Denver's record has lived at the airport since DIA opened in 1995; before that it was kept at Stapleton, much closer to downtown — one reason old-timers' memories of "Denver weather" don't always match the modern station.

The airport's setting matters. It sits out on open prairie, away from downtown's heat island and away from the foothills. On a calm, clear night it radiates heat and starts cold; under a strong sun at altitude it can warm fast. A reading from downtown, a suburb or your car is measuring somewhere else, sometimes by several degrees. When someone asks how hot Denver got, the official answer is the airport's number in the climate report.

THE RULEDenver's official high is Denver International's high, as printed in the NWS climate report — preliminary in the late afternoon, final after midnight.

DENVER'S SEESAW: CHINOOKS, UPSLOPE, AND THE 25-DEGREE WEEK

Denver's unique microclimates are largely shaped by its high-desert, semi-arid environment and the imposing presence of the Rocky Mountains. Situated at an altitude of about 5,280 feet, the city experiences thin air that allows rapid solar heating during the day and swift radiative cooling after sunset, resulting in significant daily temperature fluctuations.

Additionally, the surrounding mountains play a major role in local weather patterns. While the Rockies can create a partial rain shadow effect that blocks certain storm tracks and preserves abundant sunshine, they also interact dynamically with approaching cold fronts and upslope weather systems, creating localized atmospheric variations across the metro area.

Put those together and Denver's temperature becomes a tug-of-war between the mountains and the Arctic. Three forces do most of the pulling:

  • Downslope warming. When wind comes over the Rockies from the west, it descends roughly 5,000 feet to the airport and warms as it sinks — the Chinook effect. Downslope days can run 10 to 20 degrees warmer than the forecast's calm-day logic, spike the high early, or rescue a cold day late.
  • Upslope flow. When the wind turns easterly, it pushes plains air up against the foothills. The result is cloud, fog and a hard cap on warming — the airport can sit in gray chill while stations an hour east are in the sun.
  • Frontal crashes. Cold fronts arrive from the north with little to slow them down. A front at 1 PM ends the day's climb on the spot, and the morning's modest high becomes the official one.

You don't need to take the seesaw on faith — it's in this week's table above. Friday November 20 printed 65°, fourteen degrees above normal, on a downslope push that set the high by 12:10 PM. By Monday November 23 the high was 40°, ten below normal. Twenty-five degrees of peak-to-peak swing in four days, at one station, in one week. That is the Denver market in a single picture: the bracket you loved on Friday is a different climate by Monday.

THE WRONG-STATION TRAP: BOULDER IS NOT DENVER'S NUMBER

Every city on this site has a nearby station that looks like Denver and isn't. Denver's are unusually tempting, because the metro is spread along the foothills and each spot has its own weather:

  • Boulder (KBDU) sits right against the foothills, about 40 miles northwest. On downslope days Boulder is often the warmest spot in Colorado; on upslope days it's buried first. Either way, it is not the settlement station.
  • Rocky Mountain Metropolitan (KBJC) in Broomfield and Centennial (KAPA) in the south metro are real ASOS stations with real feeds — and neither one settles the Denver market.
  • Downtown sensors and phone apps blend stations and models. Downtown runs warmer at night and can differ from the airport by several degrees at the afternoon peak.

The desk learned this lesson at another airport, in Louisville, where a wrong-airport reading moved a whole market. The Denver version of the rule is the same: if the number didn't come from Denver International, it doesn't settle anything, no matter how official it looks.

THE RULEKDEN or it didn't happen. Boulder, Broomfield, Centennial and downtown are information — only the airport is settlement.

WHEN DENVER'S NUMBER BECOMES OFFICIAL

Denver's climate report comes from the National Weather Service office in Boulder, and it lands in two steps. A preliminary report posts in the late afternoon — today's came at 4:32 PM Mountain — carrying the day's high as it stands. The final report, covering the full climate day, posts after midnight, around 12:30 AM Mountain. Kalshi settles the Denver market on The Weather Company's reporting of that final number, in whole degrees.

Two habits worth building: never grade your own day off the preliminary alone, because the final is the one that pays; and remember the climate day runs midnight to midnight standard time, so during daylight saving the official day runs 1 AM to 1 AM on the clock — a warm midnight hour can belong to either day, and the report, not your watch, decides which.

QUESTIONS ABOUT DENVER'S HIGH TEMPERATURE

What is the high temperature in Denver today?

The box at the top of this page shows the high so far today at Denver International Airport, Denver's official weather station, updated from the National Weather Service's live observations. Those readings are unofficial until the climate report posts — preliminary in the late afternoon, final after midnight.

What was Denver's high temperature yesterday?

Yesterday's official Denver high is shown in the top box and in the tables above, straight from the NWS climate report for Denver International.

Where is Denver's official temperature measured?

At Denver International Airport, station code KDEN, on the plains about 25 miles northeast of downtown, at 5,434 feet. Denver's official daily highs and records come from this station.

What time of day does Denver usually hit its high?

On a typical fall day, between about 12:02 PM and 3:51 PM, most often in the 3 PM hour. But about one November day in seven sets its high before noon, when a front arrives early or a downslope wind spikes the morning.

Why is Denver's forecast so often off by ten degrees?

Because Denver's weather genuinely swings that much. In the official record, November highs at the airport average about 55°, but the standard deviation is 13.4° — a 40° day and a 71° day are both normal November outcomes. The forecast is usually a fair center; the range around it is just wider here than almost anywhere else.

Why doesn't my weather app match Denver's official high?

Your app is usually showing a forecast, a model estimate for your location, or a different sensor — often a downtown or foothills reading. Only Denver International's reading becomes the official high.

When does the official Denver high come out?

A preliminary climate report usually posts around 4:30 PM Mountain. The final report, with the official high for the full climate day, posts after midnight, around 12:30 AM Mountain.
