WEEK 4, PART 4: WHAT WE CHANGED

By Buzz·PUBLISHED Tuesday, November 24, 2026·UPDATED November 24, 2026·6 MIN READ

Fifteen days, one model, and a rulebook that got rewritten in public. This is every major change we made during Paper Mode v1 — what prompted it, what the graded record said, and the two changes we looked at hard and refused to make.

Key insights
  • The forecast's center became NOAA's National Blend of Models on Nov 2 — and every later change assumed it.
  • The honesty machinery came before the fixes: a permanent market shadow, a named reason required for every pick, and a rule that dead picks are shown, never hidden.
  • Our biggest mid-run change — tightening the model toward the market — produced Scout's two worst days ever (0-for-24, twice). We reversed it within 48 hours and published why.
  • The 9¢ floor came straight from the graded log: picks under 9¢ won 2.4% of the time and lost $350.10. Picks at 20¢+ were the only profitable band of the run.
  • The change we refused: no exit rule. The replays never produced one that made the book profitable, so everything rode to settlement — and still does until a rule earns its way in.

FIRST, BETTER INPUTS (NOV 2)

Two changes on the same day set up everything after.

The forecast center became the National Blend of Models — NOAA's blend of the major weather models — using its percentile spread (P10/P50/P90) pulled from the official NOMADS bulletin, replacing our earlier four-model median. If the center and the spread are going to drive every probability we state, they should come from the best public source there is, not from our own average of averages.

And every candidate pick started getting checked, at scan time, against the settlement station's observed high-so-far. A bracket the day has already beaten isn't a longshot; it's dead. At first those flags were information only — picks stayed tracked exactly as selected, flags and all. The display rule they grew into is below.

THEN, THE HONESTY MACHINERY (NOV 3–5)

Before fixing anything else, we built the instruments that would tell us the truth about the fixes.

  • The market shadow (Nov 3). A third portfolio that buys the market's favorite bracket in every city, same $3 ticket, forever. It's a benchmark, never an input — it exists so "did we beat the market?" always has a graded answer. (It won the run. Part 2 of this series has the scoreboard.)
  • Ten amendments (Nov 4). Among them: the numeric exit test that Parts 2 and 3 report on; the Trade Confidence Score retired as a selection input — a number that felt like knowledge and wasn't; a named mispricing required for every pick — a reason tag plus one checkable sentence, locked at entry, so no pick gets to be a vibe; far-from-center picks flagged into a LOTTERY tracking class; a calibration log recording every stated probability against its settlement; and a shadow ledger of intraday bids and asks on every position — the raw material for the exit research.
  • The display rule (Nov 5). Picks flagged dead at scan are never hidden and never shown as recommendations. They sit in a labeled quarantine on the board, and they count as losses in the record. Hiding them would make the numbers prettier and the site worthless. The unfiltered record is the product.

THE RHYTHM (NOV 8–12)

Fall highs print early, and a desk that scans at 10 AM is grading a day that's already half-decided. The scan moved to 5 AM. The disagreement pass — where Bright's recentered model argues with Scout's slate — got a name (the Genuine Recentered Distribution Method) and a fixed slot. And on Nov 12 the six Pacific cities got their own later pass at 3:45 PM ET, because their 11 AM reads were all overnight prints — and all losing. We also tested a 2:45 AM scan; it graded a touch better, but the difference traced to the forecast cycle, not the hour, so we closed the experiment and changed nothing. Not every improvement is real, and that one wasn't.

THE TIGHTENING, AND THE REVERSAL (NOV 8–10)

The centerpiece — because it's the change that hurt.

On Nov 8 we tightened the system: a dead-bracket filter, a data-error rule for absurd prices, settle-grade observations from two independent feeds, and a market anchor — our probabilities shrunk 10% toward the market's on every pick, on the theory that the crowd's sharpness (see Part 3) should temper ours.

The graded answer came back in 48 hours: 0-for-24 on Nov 9. 0-for-24 again on Nov 10. The anchored slates collapsed into penny longshots — the median ticket on Nov 10 cost 2¢ — because shrinking toward the market pushed our picks off our own model's center and into brackets nobody sane would buy at any confidence. The theory sounded prudent. The record called it the two worst days of the run.

So on Nov 10 the anchor came off — selection went back to the raw model probability, the same day, in the open. And the reversal shipped with a partner change that the graded log had been shouting about for a week:

The 9¢ floor. No candidate under 9¢, for Scout or for disagreement picks. The evidence, straight from the calibration log (Oct 29 – Nov 9): picks under 9¢ were 212 picks with 5 wins — 2.4% — and −$350.10. Picks at 20¢ and up were 51 picks, 20 wins — 39.2% — and +$6.81, the only profitable band of the entire run. Cheap tickets weren't value; they were a leak with good marketing.

What followed is in Part 2: four straight green days to close the run, including Scout's best day of the experiment. Same model. Different rules about what it's allowed to buy.

WHAT WE REFUSED TO CHANGE

Two refusals, both deliberate.

No exit rule. The shadow ledger let us replay the whole run against the alternatives — time exits, fixed-percentage cash-outs, trailing stops. A trailing stop was the most consistent candidate, ahead of riding on all five replayed days. But no rule — none — made the book profitable, and the best-looking split was fitted to the same five days it was tested on. Adopting it would have been curve-fitting dressed as discipline. So v1 rode everything to settlement, and the exit question moved to where it belongs: Paper Mode v2, where identical entries now run under different exit rules side by side, in simulation, until one earns its way in on fresh evidence.

No shading the forecast. A standing proposal all run was to nudge the model's center by a fixed amount — shade it warmer or cooler and let the brackets follow. It was rejected on the record on Nov 4 and stayed rejected: a fixed nudge is a superstition with a decimal point. The center comes from the Blend; if the Blend is biased somewhere, that's a research question with data attached, not a thumb on the scale.

WHERE THE METHOD STANDS

Everything above — the Blend anchor, the raw probabilities, the floor, the flags, the shadow — still runs today, unchanged, as the control system inside Paper Mode v2. The arenas around it are testing the questions v1 couldn't answer. When something in the method changes from here, it will change the way these did: on graded evidence, in the open, with the old rule's record left standing next to the new one.

That closes the Week 4 series. The journal goes back to its regular rhythm from here — and the record, as always, stays public either way.
