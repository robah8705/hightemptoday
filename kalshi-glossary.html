THE WORDS ON THE SCREEN, IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Every term on a Kalshi weather market page, defined the way traders actually use it — half about how you trade, half about how the winner is decided.

By Buzz·PUBLISHED Monday, November 23, 2026·UPDATED November 23, 2026·7 MIN READ
1 · THE BASICS2 · THE PRICE MACHINERY3 · HOW THE WINNER IS DECIDED4 · THE TRADER'S MATH

KEY INSIGHTS

  • A Kalshi price is a probability in disguise: 60¢ means the market thinks it happens about 60 times in 100.
  • Weather markets don't settle on your weather app. They settle on one official number, from one named station, published in one specific report.
  • Half the vocabulary is about how you trade (orders, spreads, liquidity) and half is about how the winner is decided (settlement). Both halves matter; the second half is where new traders get surprised.
01

THE BASICS

Prediction market. A market where the thing being traded is the outcome of a future event. Prices move with what traders believe, and the winning side gets paid in full when the event resolves.

Event contract. The actual product. A contract pays $1 if the event happens as defined and $0 if it doesn't. Kalshi is a regulated exchange for event contracts — the contract's written definition, not the headline, decides who wins.

Bracket. A slice of possible outcomes. Daily high-temperature markets split the day into 2-degree brackets (78° to 79°, 80° to 81°) plus open-ended brackets at both ends (77° or below, 90° or above). You are always betting on a bracket, never on an exact temperature.

Yes and No. Every bracket can be bought from either side. Buying Yes at 40¢ pays $1 if the high lands in that bracket. Buying No at 60¢ pays $1 if it lands anywhere else. Yes and No prices on the same bracket add up to roughly $1.

Favorite and longshot. The favorite is the bracket with the highest price — the outcome the market considers most likely. A longshot is a cheap bracket, priced low because the market thinks it rarely lands. Cheap is not the same as wrong, and expensive is not the same as safe.

02

THE PRICE MACHINERY

Bid and ask. The bid is the highest price a buyer is currently offering; the ask is the lowest price a seller is currently accepting. The number on the screen is usually the midpoint or the last trade — the bid and ask are what you can actually trade at right now.

Spread. The gap between the bid and the ask. A 58¢ bid and a 63¢ ask is a 5¢ spread. Wide spreads are a real cost: buy at the ask and sell at the bid and the spread comes out of your pocket before the weather says a word.

Liquidity. How much money is actually sitting in the market, ready to trade. A liquid market has tight spreads and enough size to take your order at the quoted price. A thin market — common in smaller cities and early in the morning — can move several cents on one small order.

Limit order. An order that only fills at your price or better. You name the price; if the market never reaches it, you never trade. Most patient trading on Kalshi is limit orders resting in the book.

Market order. An order that fills immediately at the best available prices, whatever they are. Fast, and in a thin weather market, often expensive — you pay the spread and then some.

Maker and taker. A maker adds liquidity: their limit order sits in the book and waits. A taker removes it: their order matches against what's already there. The distinction matters because fees can differ by role — see Fee.

Fee. Kalshi charges a trading fee that scales with price, peaking near 50¢ and shrinking toward the extremes — the formula is in our fee calculator, which also does the break-even math. Some series also carry maker fees; daily temperature markets currently are not on Kalshi's maker-fee list.

Implied probability. The belief hidden inside a price. A 35¢ ask implies the market sees roughly a 35% chance. It's the fastest translation on the site: whenever you see a price, read it as a percentage and ask whether your own number is higher or lower.

03

HOW THE WINNER IS DECIDED

Settlement. The official determination of what happened, and who gets paid. For daily highs, settlement follows one published value — not the warmest reading anyone saw on any screen during the day. The full chain is in our settlement explainer.

Settlement station. The specific weather station named in the contract — an airport ASOS in most cities (Miami International, Dallas/Fort Worth), Central Park in New York. The city's weather is a story; the station's number is the result.

ASOS. The Automated Surface Observing System — the standardized instrument network at airports that produces the official observations everything else is built on.

Daily high. The highest temperature recorded at the settlement station across the contract's defined day (a standard-time day, not midnight-to-midnight local in the way most people assume). It is a whole degree: there are no decimals in the number that pays you.

CLI / Climatological Report. The National Weather Service's daily climate report for each station — the document that publishes the official high. Preliminary first, final after. This report is the paper trail settlement follows.

The Weather Company. Since August 14, 2026, Kalshi's daily temperature markets settle on the high as published by The Weather Company, sourced from the NWS climate report. Same station, same day — their published value is the one that counts.

DSM (Daily Summary Message). A once-a-day automated summary generated from the station's ASOS feed, carrying the day's max and min. Traders watch it as an early read on the day; it is not the settlement source. When the DSM and the CLI disagree, the CLI is the one that pays.

Peak window. The stretch of the afternoon when a station typically prints its high — shaped by sun angle, sea breezes, and each city's habits. Most of a weather-trading day is waiting for, or reacting to, the peak window.

04

THE TRADER'S MATH

Edge. The gap between your probability and the price's implied probability, after fees. If you make a bracket 40% and can buy it at 30¢, the 10-point gap is your claimed edge. No gap, no trade — that discipline is most of the game.

Break-even. The win rate a trade needs just to cover its price and fee. Buy at 40¢ and you must be right more than ~41 times in 100 (the fee moves it) or the trade loses money on average even when your forecast is decent. Our calculator does this math trade by trade.

Calibration. Whether stated probabilities match reality over many trials. A calibrated 60% forecast wins about 60 times in 100. Calibration is the honest way to grade any forecaster — markets included — and it's how this desk grades itself, in public. We published the results in our accuracy study.
