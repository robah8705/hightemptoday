KEY FACTS ABOUT OKLAHOMA CITY'S HIGH

  • One station sets the number: Will Rogers World Airport (KOKC). Not the suburbs, not the reading on your phone.
  • Oklahoma City is the wind city. Will Rogers sits on the southwest side of the metro, fully exposed to the plains, and its November days are decided by which wind is blowing — Gulf southerlies, dryline westerlies or the next norther.
  • It reports about every 5 minutes, so you can watch the afternoon climb in close to real time.

WHY OKLAHOMA CITY'S OFFICIAL HIGH COMES FROM THE AIRPORT

Oklahoma City's official weather record is kept at Will Rogers World Airport, on the city's southwest side, at about 1,295 feet on the rolling central Oklahoma plains. It's the station the National Weather Service uses for the city's daily climate report, and the one Kalshi's Oklahoma City high-temperature market names in its rules.

The airport's open-plains exposure is total. There is no lake, no mountain and no coast anywhere in its weather — just the continent, the wind and the sky. Stations across the metro share that exposure, which makes Oklahoma City's trap less about terrain than about the metro's sheer size: the same front can be an hour apart across town.

OKLAHOMA CITY: THREE WINDS AND A DRYLINE

Central Oklahoma is where the continent's air masses meet without an appointment. Moist Gulf air, dry high-plains air and Arctic air all reach Oklahoma City regularly, and the boundaries between them — fronts and the famous dryline — are the weather.

  • Southerly return flow. Strong south winds haul Gulf warmth and moisture over the plains. Those days build steadily and peak in the mid-afternoon — Oklahoma's classic mild November pattern, sometimes 20 degrees above normal.
  • The dryline. A boundary between Gulf moisture and desert air that often sets up across Oklahoma. West of it: hot, dry, windy. East of it: humid and capped. The airport can sit on either side of the line on the same afternoon, and the sides differ by ten degrees.
  • Northers. Cold fronts sweep the plains at speed. A norther through Oklahoma City drops the temperature on the hour it arrives, and the morning's reading becomes the day's high more often here than almost anywhere on the board.

You can see the shape of it in the tables above: over the last 30 days, Will Rogers World Airport's official highs ran from 42° to 81°, averaging 65.1°. That is Oklahoma City's character in one line — the rest of this page is about trading it honestly.

THE WRONG-STATION TRAP: A BIG METRO, AN HOUR-WIDE FRONT

  • Wiley Post Airport (KPWA), on the metro's northwest side, is a full ASOS station that can sit on the other side of a dryline or an hour behind a front from Will Rogers.
  • Tinker Air Force Base (KTIK) reports from the southeast side in Midwest City — same sky, different hour, different number.
  • Downtown and OU-area sensors add heat island and campus microclimates to a market that settles on an open airfield across town.

In Oklahoma City the wrong-station risk is less about the station and more about the timing: boundaries cross the metro street by street. Only one station's crossing counts.

WHEN OKLAHOMA CITY'S NUMBER BECOMES OFFICIAL

Oklahoma City's climate report comes from the National Weather Service office in Norman, and it lands in two steps. A preliminary report posts in the late afternoon — today's came at 5:11 PM — carrying the day's high as it stands. The final report, covering the full climate day, posts around 1:25 AM. Kalshi settles the Oklahoma City market on The Weather Company's reporting of that final number, in whole degrees.

Two habits worth building: never grade your own day off the preliminary alone, because the final is the one that pays; and remember the climate day runs midnight to midnight standard time, so during daylight saving the official day runs 1 AM to 1 AM on the clock — a warm midnight hour can belong to either day, and the report, not your watch, decides which.

Because boundaries are the forecast here, and boundaries are timing — a dryline ten miles west instead of east, or a front at noon instead of four, swings the high by ten degrees or more.
