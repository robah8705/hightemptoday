HOW ACCURATE ARE WEATHER PREDICTION MARKETS? WE GRADED THEM.

Every Kalshi daily high-temperature market, graded against the official settlement for 360 city-days — the favorite test, the full-ladder test, and the 87 times we disagreed with the market and scored both sides.

By Buzz — who trades these markets daily under that name in Kalshi's own chat rooms, and whose desk's complete record is published on this site. Published Monday, November 23, 2026.

KEY INSIGHTS

  • Over 360 graded city-days, the market's favorite bracket won 60.8% of the time — and its average price was ~59¢. The market said 59%, reality delivered 60.8%. That is about as calibrated as a forecast gets.
  • Scored head-to-head on the 87 brackets where our model disagreed with the market, the market's probabilities were measurably sharper than ours (Brier 0.092 vs 0.127). We publish that because it's the truth.
  • Accurate is not the same as profitable: the always-buy-the-favorite strategy won 60.8% of its days and still finished slightly down after fees. You pay for the market's accuracy in the price.
  • The misses aren't random. They cluster in thin early books, late-day spikes, and settlement quirks — the places a careful trader can actually learn.
01

WHAT "ACCURATE" EVEN MEANS FOR A PRICE

A weather app is accurate if the temperature it shows matches the thermometer. A prediction market makes a different kind of claim. A bracket priced at 60¢ is claiming: this happens about 60 times in 100. One day can't prove that right or wrong — a 60% event is supposed to fail 40 times in 100. You can only grade a market the way you grade a forecaster: across hundreds of calls, did the things it called 60% happen about 60% of the time? Statisticians call that calibration, and it's the only honest scoreboard. So we built one.

Between October 29 and November 14, 2026, this desk graded every one of Kalshi's 24 daily high-temperature markets, every day — 360 city-days — against the official settlement value. Here's what the market's prices actually did.

02

TEST 1: THE FAVORITE TEST

The simplest check: take the market's favorite bracket each day — its single most likely outcome — and see how often it won, against what it cost.

The favorite won 219 of 360 city-days: 60.8%. Its average price was ~59¢.

Read that pair of numbers again. The market charged, on average, 59 cents for things that happened 60.8% of the time. Across 360 trials, its headline probability and reality landed within two points of each other. Anyone who tells you these markets are a casino has to explain that away; anyone who tells you they're an oracle has to explain the other 39.2% — the days the favorite simply lost, as favorites priced under 100¢ are built to do.

03

TEST 2: THE FULL LADDER

A market doesn't just name a favorite; it prices every bracket, a full probability ladder for the day. Scoring all of those prices at once (a standard measure called log loss, where lower is better) across 240 city-days:

The market's ladders: 224.19. Our own model's ladders: 323.82.

Not close. The market's full-day probability distributions were substantially sharper than the model this desk built and traded on. We keep this number on the site because a desk that won't publish the tests it fails has no business publishing the ones it passes.

04

TEST 3: THE 87 DISAGREEMENTS

The sharpest test is what happens where a forecaster and the market actually disagree — that's where money changes hands. On the 87 brackets where our model took a position against the market's price, we scored both sets of probabilities with a Brier score (0 is perfect; lower is better):

The market's prices on those brackets: Brier 0.0922 · log loss 0.3271. Our model's stated probabilities: Brier 0.1267 · log loss 0.4316.

And the calibration detail that stings: on those brackets, we stated an average probability of 21.1%. They actually won 13.8% of the time — we were overconfident by 7.3 points. The market's implied probability on the same brackets was 13.9%. Nearly exact. When we thought the market was wrong, most of the time it wasn't; we were.

05

ACCURATE IS NOT THE SAME AS PROFITABLE

Profitable? Not by itself. Our grading included a "market shadow" that mechanically bought the favorite every day at the market's price. It won 60.8% of its days — and finished the run down $21.95 after fees on $3 paper tickets. The accuracy was real, and it was already in the price. Buying correct opinions at full price is a way to lose slowly.

Beatable? In spots, and the spots have a pattern. Across the graded record, the market's misses cluster where you'd expect a crowd to be weakest: early in the morning, when books are thin and prices are set by few hands; on days when a late spike rewrites the high after prices have settled into a story; and around the settlement process itself, where the number that pays is one station's official print — not the warmest reading on anyone's app. None of that makes the market dumb. It makes it a strong baseline with specific, learnable soft spots — which is a very different thing from a slot machine, and also a very different thing from a crystal ball.

The bottom line: Kalshi's weather prices, graded over hundreds of settlements, are well calibrated: what they say happens, happens at about the stated rate. If you want one number to carry away, it's 59¢ → 60.8%. If you trade these markets, your job is not to out-forecast that baseline everywhere — nobody we've measured does, including us. Your job is to find the minority of brackets where it's wrong, size them honestly, and let the fees math veto the rest. That's the whole game, and it's harder than it sounds — our own graded record, losers included, is published on this site if you'd like to watch us keep trying.

06

QUESTIONS

Are weather prediction markets more accurate than weather forecasts?
They answer different questions. A forecast gives you a temperature; the market gives you a probability for each possible temperature, and it updates all day as traders react to new prints. Graded on calibration, the market's probabilities are excellent. Graded as a single "what will the high be" pick, its favorite lands about 61% of the time.

Does a 90¢ bracket mean it's a sure thing?
It means the market believes it happens about 90 times in 100 — which includes the other 10. Over our graded run, heavily priced favorites lost regularly, at roughly the rate their prices admitted. A 90¢ price is a strong opinion, not a guarantee.

If the market is this accurate, why trade at all?
Because accuracy is an average, and trades are specific. The market can be right 61% of the time overall and still misprice this bracket, in this city, on this kind of day. Finding those cases — and proving it over a graded record instead of a feeling — is the entire discipline.

How did you grade this?
Every market was scored against the official settlement value from the station's climate report — the same number Kalshi pays on. Our full method, including how we score probabilities, is on the methodology page.
