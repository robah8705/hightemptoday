Chicago didn't just lead the day; it outweighed the runners-up combined — 202,531 contracts, more than Los Angeles and New York together — 28 of every 100 contracts on the tape. Remember that shape when a quiet city's tiny tape gets cited as "smart money moving": in most of these markets there is barely any money, smart or otherwise.

The day's real risk wasn't in either table — it was in the accumulations, and Chicago held both ends of it. WHAT THIS MEANS — AND WHAT IT DOESN'T

  • It doesn't mean "the favorites are the play now." The 40–89¢ band won 80.6% of its markets across Nov 12–14 and lost 5.44¢ per contract doing it; on Wednesday it won 87.5% and made 5.99¢. The band is a thermometer for the kind of day it was — when favorites romp and the size sits on the right brackets, it prints; when the size leans into the one favorite that dies, it pays. One warm day doesn't repeal the earlier three.
  • It does mean a 0-for-232,076 is possible. Nearly a third of Wednesday's contracts were bought at 2¢ or less, and not one of them won. That isn't bad luck — the 1–2¢ band's markets were priced at 1.2% and delivered 0.0%, which over 136 markets is simply the price being right. The lottery drawer is a standing cost the crowd pays for the screenshot when one finally hits.
  • It means the piles are still not information. The day's biggest pile — 58,786 contracts — lost $7,525 sitting one bracket below the winner, in the same city where the winning pile sat exactly on the settled number. A one-sided pile is a crowd agreeing with itself at volume. Flow is a record of what people did. Settlement is the record of what was true. This page exists to keep the two in that order.
  • It means the surest trade on the tape isn't a forecast. The biggest dollar print of the day was placed at 12:02 AM, after Chicago's high was already in the books, and it still only cleared 13¢ per contract. Certainty is a business with thin margins and no opinions — which is exactly why it works, and why it will never be the screenshot anybody shares.
  • And size still needs a dollar sign. "10,000 contracts" and "one hundred dollars" were the same trade on Wednesday, three times in two minutes. Any whale-watching that counts contracts without pricing them will show you stubs and call them whales — which is, not coincidentally, how most whale content works.

For the mechanics underneath all of it: how these markets settle is the referee every number above answers to, and the fee calculator prices what flow like this paid for the privilege — remember, every P&L figure on this page is before fees, and Wednesday's collective +$314 does not survive them. Our own picks get graded with the same lack of mercy in the journal.

HOW THIS PAGE IS MADE — THE GAP, AND THE HOLE, CLOSED

The tape is Kalshi's own public trade record for the 24 daily-high temperature markets — every trade, pulled in full, de-duplicated trade by trade, then matched market by market against Kalshi's published settlement results. No market is included until its result is official, and no number on this page is estimated.

The gap: this page's last edition covered Nov 12–14, and the daily editions after it were not published — the feature sat for eleven days. Rather than drip the stale days out one at a time, it resumes here with the most recent complete day, Wednesday's tape, graded in full. The missed days stay missed, and the gap is stated on the page instead of papered over: a record that skips its own gaps would be doing the thing this feature exists to catch.

The hole in Friday, closed: the last edition left six of Friday Nov 13's New York markets unscored — 21,248 trades — because Kalshi had posted no official result for them. Those results have since been posted; all six markets now read finalized. The hole is closed, and Friday's figures will be restated with New York included the next time those days are republished — the promise from the last edition stands until they are.

A correction, in the open. Re-pulling Monday's tape for comparison, we found that Kalshi's feed had returned about 4% of Monday's trades twice inside our pull, inflating the totals in our first recap. Monday's corrected figures are 51,211 trades and 807,402 contracts across 134 markets — first published as 53,299 and 815,727. The restated scoreboard moves by pennies (the 10–39¢ band was −9.81¢ per contract, not −9.78¢; the 40–89¢ band was +9.65¢, not +9.67¢) and none of Monday's conclusions change. Every recap from today forward is de-duplicated trade by trade before scoring — and this note stays up, because a feature whose whole pitch is the graded record has to grade itself too.

Why it publishes in the early afternoon: western markets settle late. A day's tape cannot be fully scored until the last results post. A recap claiming to be final at 9 AM would be inventing the western third of its own story — so this one waits, and says so.
