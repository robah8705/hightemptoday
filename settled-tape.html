THREE DAYS IN NUMBERS: WHERE THE VOLUME LIVED

Los Angeles was the loudest market of the three days — the only city above 400,000 contracts — with New York second despite sitting out Friday entirely. The three days had different shapes: Thursday was a New York–Chicago argument (349,109 contracts between them), Friday belonged to the West with New York missing, and Saturday ran through Miami and Austin, where the day's biggest tail piles formed and died.

Day by day: Thursday ran 52,902 trades and 1,045,114 contracts across 144 markets — the heaviest day of the three. Friday ran 40,375 trades and 871,930 contracts across the 137 markets that have official results; New York's six markets and their 21,248 trades are excluded, which is why Friday's totals look light next to Thursday's despite Friday's tape being the rawest of the three before exclusions. Volume still follows argument. The cities with two-sided weather stories are where contracts change hands; the settled, obvious cities barely trade. Remember that shape when a quiet city's tiny tape gets cited as "smart money moving": in most of these markets there is barely any money, smart or otherwise.

THE SCOREBOARD: DID THE FLOW POINT AT THE WINNER?

The three days, band by band:

  • Thursday: the mid-band won at almost exactly its price (22.5% of markets against 22.4% implied) and still made +13.56¢ per contract — its winning markets carried the size. The 40–89¢ band also won at its price (71.0% against 70.8%) and lost −9.06¢ per contract, for the mirror-image reason. Even the cheap seats had a day: 3–9¢ flow won 10.1% against a 5.4% price.
  • Friday: the favorites romped and the underdogs were buried. The 40–89¢ band won 92.3% of its markets against a 72.1% price — its one money day of the three, +4.23¢ — while the 10–39¢ band won just 13.0% against 22.3% and lost −2.66¢. Everything under 10¢ won literally nothing: 0.0% in both cheap bands, all day.
  • Saturday: Thursday's shape, louder. The 10–39¢ band made +16.49¢ per contract (26.8% won against 23.4% implied); the 40–89¢ band won 79.1% of its markets against 69.5% implied and lost −9.58¢ per contract anyway.
  • 90¢ and up: 99.7% across the three days — and Friday's single miss cost the band its perfection and its profit (−1.11¢ that day). One Houston print is the whole story of that row.

The combined record — a measured record, every trade graded against the settled result of its market — is not a rating of anybody's skill and not a hint about tomorrow:

A scoreboard that only counts wins will tell you the crowd is sharp. A scoreboard that counts money says otherwise.

HOW WE SCOREThe taker side of every trade is graded against its market's settled result. Win rate is counted once per market — the dominant taker side — never per contract, so a single huge print can't stuff the ballot. P&L is per contract, before fees. Buckets with too few markets are flagged, not hidden: Friday's $500-and-up row rested on only 7 markets, and Thursday's on 20; the three-day row above rests on 38 and is the first one worth reading.
04

WHAT THIS MEANS — AND WHAT IT DOESN'T

  • It doesn't mean "buy the 10–39¢ band." That band was the worst seat in the house on Monday (−9.81¢ per contract) and Friday (−2.66¢), and the best on Wednesday, Thursday, and Saturday. What the band does is amplify the kind of day it was: when official prints land a degree above the live feed, mid-priced underdogs feast; when favorites romp, they starve. The band is a thermometer, not a strategy.
  • It does mean the favorite band's wins are worth less than they look. Three days, 201 markets: the 40–89¢ flow beat its implied price by nearly ten points of win rate and still paid out 5.44¢ per contract for the privilege. If you only ever see win rates — and win rates are what gets screenshotted — you will systematically overrate the crowd's favorite side. Count the money.
  • It means the piles are still not information. Twelve biggest piles across three days, ten losses — and the two wins were a contested bracket's winning side and a pile that sat exactly on the settled number. A one-sided pile is a crowd agreeing with itself at volume. Flow is a record of what people did. Settlement is the record of what was true. This page exists to keep the two in that order.
  • It means the last cent has a price. The 99¢ Houston buy was not stupid — the same trade won five times on Thursday alone and dozens of times across these three days. It's a business that collects $20 at a time and occasionally pays $1,980. Anyone showing you the wins without the tail is showing you half a business.
  • And size still needs a dollar sign. The biggest contract print of three days cost $250. "25,000 contracts" and "two hundred fifty dollars" are the same trade. Any whale-watching that counts contracts without pricing them will show you stubs and call them whales — which is, not coincidentally, how most whale content works.

For the mechanics underneath all of it: how these markets settle is the referee every number above answers to — Friday's Houston print was, more than anything, a story about that referee — and the fee calculator prices what flow like this paid for the privilege. Our own picks get graded with the same lack of mercy in the journal.

HOW THIS PAGE IS MADE — THE GAP, THE HOLE, AND A CORRECTION

The tape is Kalshi's own public trade record for the 24 daily-high temperature markets — every trade, pulled in full, de-duplicated trade by trade, then matched market by market against Kalshi's published settlement results. No market is included until its result is official, and no number on this page is estimated.

Why three days at once: this page missed its Thursday, Friday, and Saturday editions. Rather than drip them out stale, one at a time, they publish together here, each day's figures kept separate wherever the days disagree — which, as the scoreboard shows, is often. The normal cadence resumes with the next edition: yesterday's tape, graded and final, early each afternoon.

The hole in Friday: as of Sunday night, November 15, six of Friday's New York markets still show no official result on Kalshi — the market pages read "closed," with no result posted. Those six markets carry 21,248 of Friday's trades, including the day's largest two-sided fight (New York's "49° or below" bracket alone drew roughly 366,000 contracts of raw flow). Friday's recap is therefore scored on its 137 settled markets only, and New York does not appear in Friday's city figures. If Kalshi posts the results, Friday's numbers will be restated on this page and the restatement will say so.

A correction, in the open. Re-pulling Monday's tape for comparison, we found that Kalshi's feed had returned about 4% of Monday's trades twice inside our pull, inflating the totals in our first recap. Monday's corrected figures are 51,211 trades and 807,402 contracts across 134 markets — first published as 53,299 and 815,727. The restated scoreboard moves by pennies (the 10–39¢ band was −9.81¢ per contract, not −9.78¢; the 40–89¢ band was +9.65¢, not +9.67¢) and none of Monday's conclusions change. Every recap from today forward is de-duplicated trade by trade before scoring — and this note stays up, because a feature whose whole pitch is the graded record has to grade itself too.

Why it publishes in the early afternoon: western markets settle late. A day's tape cannot be fully scored until the last results post. A recap claiming to be final at 9 AM would be inventing the western third of its own story — so this one waits, and says so.
