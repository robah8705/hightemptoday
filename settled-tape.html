The top two rows are one moment, and it's the day's neatest story. At 3:37 PM ET, within seconds, someone bought 5,000 contracts of Miami 85–86° at 1¢ and 5,000 contracts against Miami 83–84° at 1¢ — lottery tickets on both sides of the bracket in between. Miami's official high landed in exactly that in-between bracket, 83–84°. Both tickets lost. The day paid the seat nobody in that flurry bought.

Three identical $1,980 prints, all buying against a bracket at 99¢ after that bracket's weather was effectively decided — roughly $1,980 at risk to collect roughly $20, three times over. That's not forecasting. That's settlement arbitrage — picking up the last cent on a decided market — and for the second straight recap it's what most genuine size in these markets actually is. Every one of the day's five biggest real-money prints won, and none of them knew anything about tomorrow's weather.

The day's biggest accumulations tell the opposite story, louder than Monday's. Across the full session, 56,061 taker-Yes contracts piled into Los Angeles 85° to 86° — the largest single bracket-and-side pile in any city — and it lost: Los Angeles settled at 83–84°. The next three biggest piles lost too: New York 61–62° (45,638 contracts; New York settled 63–64°), Los Angeles 82° or below (33,379), and Dallas 78–79° (26,946). A pile that big feels like information. On Wednesday it was just a crowd, agreeing loudly, in the wrong direction — four times over.

THE SCOREBOARD: DID THE FLOW POINT AT THE WINNER?

The short version — by price:

  • Under 10¢: still the cheap seats. The 1–2¢ flow won nothing at all; the 3–9¢ flow won 2.4% against a 5.4% implied price.
  • 10–39¢: the day's winner — 28.2% of markets won against a 21.9% price, +10.87¢ per contract. The exact band that was Monday's worst.
  • 40–89¢: won 83.6% of markets against a 72.1% price — and still lost money per contract (−2.81¢). Both things are true; the second one is the one that pays.
  • 90¢ and up: perfect again, 100% against 97.3% implied. That's the late-day "almost settled" flow, which proves nothing.

Why did the mid-band feast on Wednesday after bleeding on Monday? Because Wednesday's weather kept breaking upward at the official print. HOW WE SCOREThe taker side of every trade is graded against its market's settled result. Win rate is counted once per market — the dominant taker side — never per contract, so a single huge print can't stuff the ballot. P&L is per contract, before fees. Buckets with too few markets are flagged, not hidden: the $500+ row above rests on only 12 markets and proves nothing yet.

WHAT THIS MEANS — AND WHAT IT DOESN'T

  • It doesn't mean "buy the 10–39¢ band." That band was the worst seat in the house on Monday (−9.81¢ per contract) and the best on Wednesday (+10.87¢). Two days, two opposite answers — which is the actual answer: a single day's flow scoreboard is weather, not climate. Anyone selling you a winning band off one day is selling you noise.
  • It does mean the piles are not information. For the second straight recap, the day's largest accumulation lost — and this time the top four all lost. Flow is a record of what people did. Settlement is the record of what was true. This page exists to keep the two in that order.
  • It means size still needs a dollar sign. "5,000 contracts" and "$50.00" are the same trade. Any whale-watching that counts contracts without pricing them will show you stubs and call them whales — which is, not coincidentally, how most whale content works.
  • And the real money is still the boring money. The day's five biggest dollar prints all bought near-certainties at 99¢ and all won about 1% on their stake. That's a business, not a prophecy — and by the time a print exists to follow, the information in it has been spent. There is no live version of this page, on purpose.

For the mechanics underneath all of it: how these markets settle is the referee every number above answers to — Wednesday's mid-band feast was, more than anything, a story about that referee — and the fee calculator prices what flow like Wednesday's paid for the privilege. Our own picks get graded with the same lack of mercy in the journal.

HOW THIS PAGE IS MADE — AND A CORRECTION TO THE FIRST ONE

The tape is Kalshi's own public trade record for the 24 daily-high temperature markets — every trade, pulled in full, then matched market by market against Kalshi's published settlement results. Wednesday's recap covers 52,619 trades across 142 markets; no market is included until its result is official, and no number on this page is estimated.

A correction, in the open. Re-pulling Monday's tape for comparison, we found that Kalshi's feed had returned about 4% of Monday's trades twice inside our pull, inflating the totals in our first recap. Monday's corrected figures are 51,211 trades and 807,402 contracts across 134 markets — first published as 53,299 and 815,727. The restated scoreboard moves by pennies (the 10–39¢ band was −9.81¢ per contract, not −9.78¢; the 40–89¢ band was +9.65¢, not +9.67¢) and none of Monday's conclusions change. Every recap from today forward is de-duplicated trade by trade before scoring — and this note stays up, because a feature whose whole pitch is the graded record has to grade itself too.

Why it publishes in the early afternoon: western markets settle late. Wednesday's tape could not be fully scored until the last results posted on Thursday. A recap claiming to be final at 9 AM would be inventing the western third of its own story — so this one waits, and says so. That's the cadence to expect: yesterday's tape, graded and final, early each afternoon.
